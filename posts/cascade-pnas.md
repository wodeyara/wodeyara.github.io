A paper I have been working on for a few years is finally published! It came out last month in PNAS and is titled: "<a href="https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2517454123" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreffer">A hierarchical cascade of sleep rhythms supports motor memory and is hijacked by epileptic spikes in human epilepsy</a>" [<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40654700/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreffer">Free Version</a>]

This paper was both a joy and a terror to work on. I loved staying with the data and puzzling out what is happening in the epileptic #brain during #sleep, but also in a healthy brain. The window I like looking through (#eeg or #lfp) shows #oscillations - periodic fluctuations in voltage measured from neuronal populations. 

I am grateful I could bring this to a conclusion. The first person to thank is my partner (and my 2-year old), because there were a fair few late evenings, weekends, and even vacations spent on it. I transitioned to an assistant professor position along the way, so no surprise it took so long. And I am also  grateful to my mentors Mark Kramer and Catherine Chu for sticking with it and ensuring it got done!

So, what did we actually find?
First, we looked across three brain regions believed to orchestrate the key oscillatory players of sleep: Frontal cortex for slow oscillations (0.5 - 2 Hz), #thalamus for #spindles (9 - 15 Hz) and #hippocampus for #ripples (in humans: 70 - 150 Hz). Curiously, we saw all three oscillations in all three regions - expected for slow oscillations and spindles, but ripples in thalamus remain an interesting mystery (healthy? unhealthy? what is their function? - well, what is the function of any of these oscillations really - we don't know yet).

Second, when we aligned all oscillations to the frontal slow oscillations and looked at cross-correlation histograms, we saw a cascade, likely hierarchical. After the trough of a slow oscillation, thalamic spindles appear, then frontal and hippocampal ones, with ripples across regions alongside [more on this in the paper].

Third, for these co-occurrences of [assumed] healthy oscillations we had dropped epileptic spikes. For the last part of the story we brought them back. (Side note: "Thalamic epileptic spikes disrupt sleep spindles in patients with EE" - prior work, link below). And we stayed totally agnostic about the predictors: all combinations between oscillations, and between oscillations and spikes.

Turned out that when predicting motor #memory #consolidation (how much better can you type a pre-defined sequence the morning after training?), spikes alone poorly predict disruption, but when they occur alongside slow oscillations, the coupled rates strongly predicted disrupted consolidation. There has been work pointing this way, but this is the first time we've seen it alongside a measurement of overnight consolidation in humans! In fact, a joint PLS model across all predictors explained 70% of the variance.

There is, of course, much more to be said, but it's in the paper!
