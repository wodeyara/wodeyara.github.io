Epilepsy is frequently thought about from the perspective of seizures, with the primary motivation of therapy being seizure reduction. But there is another dimension to epilepsy that can sometimes be even more disabling. Epileptic spikes — transient voltage bursts that occur *between* seizures — can progressively impair cognitive function.

In a recent *Brain* paper, we showed that epileptic spikes during sleep originate in the cortex and may be facilitated by a widespread sleep rhythm called the **slow oscillation**. When these spikes reach the **thalamus** — a deeply interconnected structure with broad modulatory roles — they disrupt **sleep spindles**, the localized sleep rhythms that have been linked to overnight memory consolidation through their role in neural plasticity. A steady degradation of spindles therefore offers a plausible mechanism for the cognitive decline seen in epileptic encephalopathy.

The work also clarifies why spindles sometimes appear right after a spike: slow oscillations facilitate both, yet when a spike reaches the thalamus, the spindle is disrupted.

Methodologically, we treated slow oscillations, spindles, and spikes as point events and used **Poisson generalized linear models** to capture rhythmic, context-dependent effects — modelling how thalamic spike propagation modulates the influence of the slow oscillation on spindles.
